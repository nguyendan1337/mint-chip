Market today

Market's mixed. The market has been choppy for weeks: tech corrected into November, then bounced back hard on election results and falling oil, leaving the broad market higher but still working through high rates and an expensive AI trade.

Election results spark a relief rally Voters handed Democrats control of both houses of Congress on November 3, and stocks rallied hard on November 4, with the Dow up about 1,500 points intraday and the S&P 500 up about 1.7%, while the 10-year Treasury yield slipped to about 4.90%. Divided government usually means less new spending, which markets read as less pressure on borrowing and inflation, and defense and data-center stocks fell as investors priced in tougher scrutiny of both.

Oil plunges on peace-talk hopes Crude plunged about 7.6% on November 2 to about $105 a barrel for Brent as Qatar-mediated talks raised hopes of reopening the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway carrying a large share of world oil shipments. Cheaper oil means cheaper gasoline and less inflation pressure, which is why the whole market cheered, but no deal has actually been reached, and tanker attacks continued into November 3, so the relief could reverse fast if talks break down.

The AI trade whipsaws the market The Nasdaq-100 entered a technical correction on November 2 as investors questioned whether huge AI spending by Big Tech is earning its keep, then chip stocks rebounded 3% on November 3 after AMD beat expectations and forecast faster 2027 data-center growth. The AI trade is now the market's swing factor: strong earnings lift everything, spending doubts knock everything down, and that whiplash is why the market feels jumpy even on good days.

High bond yields keep competing with stocks Safe government bonds still pay close to 5%, with the 10-year yield near 4.89% and the Fed's target rate at 3.75-4.00%, so bonds compete with stocks for investor money and keep borrowing expensive for companies and homebuyers. That is the quiet headwind behind every rally: as long as bonds pay this much, stocks need genuinely strong earnings to justify their prices.

Jobs and inflation data arrive this week The October jobs report lands Friday, November 6, and October consumer prices follow Tuesday, November 10, with September inflation already running 3.7% and September payrolls at just 29,000. A hot inflation or strong jobs print would push interest-rate cuts further out and lift bond yields, hurting rate-sensitive stocks, while soft prints would do the reverse, so the next week of data matters more than today's rally.

So what: own cash flows, not headlines Today's surge is real but narrow in its cause: an election result plus an oil drop, layered over a market that just corrected. The names that keep working are the ones with cash flows and guidance to back their runs, not the ones riding a headline, which is why this page still leaves slots empty rather than filling them with hopeful stories.

About Mint. Mint looks for American stocks and ETFs that have already proven themselves — names that beat their benchmark over the past year. For stocks that benchmark is VGT, a technology index; for ETFs it's VOO, which tracks the S&P 500. Then Mint asks the harder question: which are most likely to keep winning without stumbling right after you buy?

The philosophy is simple: avoid losses first. We look past recent gains to the business behind each name, the risks that could derail it, and whether its success looks durable or depends on hype, headlines, or a one-time event. Strong recent performance gets a name noticed. Strong evidence earns it a spot. If nothing clears the bar, we leave the slot empty — every pick earns its place.

How it works, in three stages:

Find strength. Every stock and ETF is screened for beating its benchmark over the past year, then scored on the quality of the business, the timing of entry, and the steadiness of the ride. Hard risk gates throw out anything too volatile or too deeply scarred.

Question the story. Survivors get researched — we read the news behind each name, not just the numbers. How much does the story depend on things outside the company's control: wars, hype, one-time windfalls? Too much, and it's out on the spot. For the rest: how likely is the strength to continue, and how much do we trust our own read?

Weigh the odds. Will the strength continue, or reverse? Those judgments become one number — expected value: the likely gains if it holds, minus the likely pain if it turns, adjusted for how much we trust our own read.

Mint is built around a simple idea: find strength, question the story, weigh the odds, and only make room for what earns it.

Not financial advice. Past performance doesn't predict future returns. Also: there are gremlins that have control over the markets — they hate you personally, and they do the exact opposite of your buys and sells purely to spite you. Invest accordingly.
