Market today

Market's down. The market has been sliding for three straight days and the Nasdaq is now in a correction, more than 10 percent below its October high, as investors sell AI stocks ahead of Nvidia earnings on Tuesday and worry that high bond yields will stay high.

Tech is taking the beating Stocks fell again on Monday, with the S&P 500 down about 1.9 percent and the Nasdaq down about 2.5 percent, while chip stocks dropped more than 5 percent. The selling is concentrated in the AI names that led the market up, and it deepened after Nvidia lost its chief executive last week, days before its earnings report.

Safe bonds pay a lot right now The 10-year Treasury yield sits near 5.1 percent, which means a risk-free government bond pays enough to compete with stocks for investor money. With inflation still warm, some forecasters now expect the Federal Reserve to raise rates again in December, and that possibility pulls money out of the market, especially out of expensive growth stocks.

Everyday businesses are holding up The damage is not spread evenly. Drug distributors, hospitals, insurers and food and household-goods companies kept reporting steady growth and raised outlooks through the selloff, because people need medicine, coverage and groceries in any market. Equal-weighted indexes fell far less than the headline indexes, which shows the average stock is doing better than the biggest tech names.

Oil has calmed down Oil has fallen roughly a fifth in November as shipments through the Strait of Hormuz largely recovered and forecasters see more supply than demand next quarter. Cheaper energy takes pressure off household budgets and inflation, even though fresh tanker incidents this weekend show the war risk is not gone.

The bottom line This is a fearful market priced for bad news from one company on Tuesday. The picks lean into businesses whose customers show up regardless: medicine distribution, home services, insurance and the broad market itself at low cost, and they avoid names whose story depends on the AI trade calming down.

About Mint. Mint looks for American stocks and funds that have already proven themselves, names beating their benchmark across a mix of recent windows: the past year, the past six months, and the past three months. For stocks that benchmark is VGT, a technology index; for funds it's VOO, which tracks the S&P 500. Then Mint asks the harder question: which are most likely to keep doing well over the next year, even if the market dips along the way?

The philosophy is simple: guard against big losses, but judge a pick by the year ahead, not by a rough month. We look past recent gains to the business behind each name, the risks that could derail it, and whether its success looks durable or depends on hype, headlines, or a one-time event. Strong recent performance gets a name noticed. Strong evidence earns it a spot. If nothing clears the bar, we leave the slot empty: every pick earns its place.

How it works, in three stages:

Find strength. Every stock and fund is screened for strength against its benchmark across those windows, then scored on the quality of the business, the timing of entry, and the steadiness of the ride. Hard risk gates bar names that have run too far too fast, fallen too hard, or swing too wildly.

Question the story. Survivors get researched. We read the news behind each name, not just the numbers. How much does the story depend on things outside the company's control: wars, hype, one-time windfalls? Too much, and it's out on the spot. For the rest: how likely is the strength to continue, and how much do we trust our own read?

Weigh the odds. For a stock, those judgments become one number, the expected return. It starts from a typical year's return, adds credit for things the company has actually done, like raising its dividend, buying back its own shares, or insiders buying with their own money, and subtracts for warning signs like a big acquisition or a buyback that only covered new shares handed out. The result is scaled by how much we trust our own read and how steady the business looks. A fund's figure is different: the ETF Score, a grade from 0 to 100 against fixed standards, based on what the fund costs, how big it is, and how calm its price has been lately, meaning how little it has spiked. It is not ranked against the other funds on the page, and it is not a forecast for that one fund. A fund needs at least 50 to be picked.

Mint is built around a simple idea: find strength, question the story, weigh the odds, and only make room for what earns it.

Not financial advice. Past performance doesn't predict future returns. Also: there are gremlins that have control over the markets. They hate you personally, and they do the exact opposite of your buys and sells purely to spite you. Invest accordingly.
