Market today

Market's down. The market has fallen for three straight days and the Nasdaq is in a correction, more than 10 percent below its October high, as investors sell AI stocks ahead of Nvidia earnings tonight and brace for a likely Federal Reserve rate hike in December.

The AI selloff deepened into a correction Stocks fell again on Monday, with the S&P 500 down about 1.9 percent and chip stocks down more than 5 percent, and Tuesday brought more losses. The selling is concentrated in the AI names that carried the market all year, and tonight Nvidia reports earnings for the first time since its chief executive resigned last week, so one company's report will likely set the tone for the whole market tomorrow.

A rate hike, not a cut, is now the expected next move Strong jobs numbers and sticky inflation have traders pricing in roughly a 95 percent chance the Federal Reserve raises rates at its December 8 to 9 meeting. Safe government bonds now pay about 5.1 percent over ten years, which gives investors a real alternative to stocks and pulls money out of the market, especially out of expensive growth names.

Everyday businesses are holding up While tech falls, healthcare, food and household goods, and dividend paying value stocks have fallen far less, and healthcare is up strongly over the past month. Companies selling things people need in any economy are doing exactly what they are supposed to do in a selloff, and most of today's picks come from that side of the market.

Oil is getting cheaper even with a war on Oil settled near 93 dollars a barrel, down sharply this month, because shipments through the Strait of Hormuz have largely recovered even though attacks on tankers continue in the ninth month of the US and Iran war. Cheaper oil slowly takes pressure off inflation, which is the root of the rate worry above.

Bottom line: a defensive slate for a nervous market Today's list leans into drug distribution, insurance, healthcare, hotels and broad dividend funds, businesses with steady demand and a short distance to fall if the correction deepens. The risk to that caution is tonight: a strong Nvidia report could snap tech back quickly, and this slate would lag that rally by design.

About Mint. Mint looks for American stocks and funds that have already proven themselves, names beating their benchmark across a mix of recent windows: the past year, the past six months, and the past three months. For stocks that benchmark is VGT, a technology index; for funds it's VOO, which tracks the S&P 500. Then Mint asks the harder question: which are most likely to keep doing well over the next year, even if the market dips along the way?

The philosophy is simple: guard against big losses, but judge a pick by the year ahead, not by a rough month. We look past recent gains to the business behind each name, the risks that could derail it, and whether its success looks durable or depends on hype, headlines, or a one-time event. Strong recent performance gets a name noticed. Strong evidence earns it a spot. If nothing clears the bar, we leave the slot empty: every pick earns its place.

How it works, in three stages:

Find strength. Every stock and fund is screened for strength against its benchmark across those windows, then scored on the quality of the business, the timing of entry, and the steadiness of the ride. Hard risk gates bar names that have run too far too fast, fallen too hard, or swing too wildly.

Question the story. Survivors get researched. We read the news behind each name, not just the numbers. How much does the story depend on things outside the company's control: wars, hype, one-time windfalls? Too much, and it's out on the spot. For the rest: how likely is the strength to continue, and how much do we trust our own read?

Weigh the odds. For a stock, those judgments become one number, the expected return. It starts from a typical year's return, adds credit for things the company has actually done, like raising its dividend, buying back its own shares, or insiders buying with their own money, and subtracts for warning signs like a big acquisition or a buyback that only covered new shares handed out. The result is scaled by how much we trust our own read and how steady the business looks. A fund's figure is different: the ETF Score, a grade from 0 to 100 against fixed standards, based on what the fund costs, how big it is, and how calm its price has been lately, meaning how little it has spiked. It is not ranked against the other funds on the page, and it is not a forecast for that one fund. A fund needs at least 50 to be picked. Its bar is drawn on a scale of 200, so even the highest grade fills only half the bar.

Mint is built around a simple idea: find strength, question the story, weigh the odds, and only make room for what earns it.
