Market chat

Up, but nervously. Two engines are pulling the market: a massive AI infrastructure buildout, with big tech spending around $700 billion this year on data centers, and a Federal Reserve that just hiked in September but may be forced to pause as weak jobs data (unemployment up to 4.2%) collides with a government shutdown that's blinding it — no official jobs or inflation numbers until the lights come back on. The single biggest risk is the US-Iran war entering its eighth month: oil flows through Hormuz are recovering but refined fuel is still squeezed, and peace talks hang in the balance — an escalation reprices everything, a deal unwinds the war premium. Borrowing costs near multi-decade highs (10-year at 5.28%) are the slow grind underneath it all, punishing anything valued on distant future profits.

Financial Services

Money is pouring into asset managers — AAMI's assets hit $232.7B, up 54% in a year, with ten straight quarters of inflows, while Victory's $360B keeps beating its benchmarks. The shift to systematic investing is structural, both return excess cash to shareholders, and rising markets lift fee income twice over. A market drawdown is the risk — it would cut assets under management directly.

Technology

AI keeps expanding the attack surface, and security budgets keep growing to cover it — Fortinet's billings jumped 33% with raised guidance and a record stock price; Arrow rode distribution wins to triple-digit earnings growth. This spending is structural, not cyclical: companies can't pause security even with rates high. Both are priced for continued beats, so valuation is the check.

Industrials

No stories here, just earnings — IDEX beat and raised full-year guidance alongside its 16th straight dividend hike; Nordson raised on 35% backlog growth. Diversified order books mean growth comes from orders rather than headlines, which is the durable kind. Watch for industrial cyclicality if manufacturing softens.

Real Estate

The rooms are full — revenue per available room is up over 14% on last year, the fall convention season looks strong, fresh dividends were declared, and Park Hotels paid off a $1.275B loan. Travel demand plus cleaned-up balance sheets should carry it. Lodging is economically cyclical, so a consumer pullback is the risk.

Healthcare

The quiet compounders — Concentra has four straight earnings beats in recession-proof occupational health; Ligand beat on royalty revenue, up 34%, with all ten covering analysts rating it a Buy. Healthcare demand doesn't track the economy, and Ligand's royalties recur without it selling its own drugs. The watch items: drug-pricing pressure and Ligand's concentration in a few partnered assets.

Technology

Two structural trades in two wrappers: hyperscalers are spending around $700B this year on AI infrastructure, flowing straight into XLK's holdings, while cybersecurity spending grows about 13% as AI-driven attacks surge. A multi-year buildout (toward $3T by 2030) supports continuation. The honest risk is the ROI debate — if AI spending doesn't pay off, multiples compress across both funds.

Large Blend

Momentum itself is the trade, and it's working — both funds are up around 28% owning whatever's been winning, rebalancing into strength automatically. That self-updating is the continuation case. The failure mode is well known: violent snaps when market leadership rotates, and both carry heavy tech weight.

Mid-Cap Value

The market is paying for quality — profitable companies generating real free cash flow are being rewarded this year. Cash flow is harder to fake than earnings, which gives the strategy a structural edge. Mean reversion is the risk if speculative growth retakes market leadership.

Large Value

A value fund with a chip habit — the methodology loaded it with cheap semiconductor stocks (Micron alone is about a fifth of the fund), making it a value-tilted play on the chip cycle, up about 60%. The valuation case for chips supports continuation. But concentration cuts both ways: if memory-chip pricing rolls over, it transmits straight through to the fund.

About Mint. Mint looks for American stocks and ETFs that have already proven themselves — names that beat their benchmark over the past year. For stocks that benchmark is VGT, a technology index; for ETFs it's VOO, which tracks the S&P 500. Then Mint asks the harder question: which are most likely to keep winning without stumbling right after you buy?

The philosophy is simple: avoid losses first. We look past recent gains to the business behind each name, the risks that could derail it, and whether its success looks durable or depends on hype, headlines, or a one-time event. Strong recent performance gets a name noticed. Strong evidence earns it a spot. If nothing clears the bar, we leave the slot empty — every pick earns its place.

How it works, in three stages:

Find strength. Every stock and ETF is screened for beating its benchmark over the past year, then scored on the quality of the business, the timing of entry, and the steadiness of the ride. Hard risk gates throw out anything too volatile or too deeply scarred.

Question the story. Survivors get researched — we read the news behind each name, not just the numbers. How much does the story depend on things outside the company's control: wars, hype, one-time windfalls? Too much, and it's out on the spot. For the rest: how likely is the strength to continue, and how much do we trust our own read?

Weigh the odds. Will the strength continue, or reverse? Those judgments become one number — expected value: the likely gains if it holds, minus the likely pain if it turns, adjusted for how much we trust our own read.

Mint is built around a simple idea: find strength, question the story, weigh the odds, and only make room for what earns it.

Not financial advice. Past performance doesn't predict future returns. Also: there are gremlins that have control over the markets — they hate you personally, and they do the exact opposite of your buys and sells purely to spite you. Invest accordingly.
