Market today

Market's down. The market just had its worst week since June: the S&P 500 fell 2.7% and the Nasdaq 3.4% after Thursday's historic selloff, and Friday's bounce recovered only a fraction of it.

The jobs report was genuinely strong Employers added 165,000 jobs in October, nearly triple the 65,000 expected, unemployment fell to 4.1%, and earlier months were revised up by 84,000. A strong labor market means people keep spending, which is the foundation under corporate earnings.

Chips bounced back from the rout The Nasdaq rose 1.09% on Friday after Thursday's 5.52% plunge, its worst day in 19 months, with chip stocks up more than 4% at one point. Bargain hunters stepped in, but the bounce recovered only a small part of Thursday's losses.

The rate scare is back The hot jobs report pushed traders to price a roughly 78% chance of a December rate hike and lifted the 10-year Treasury yield to 4.86%. When safe government bonds pay nearly 5%, they compete with stocks for money, which hits growth and small companies hardest. Tuesday's inflation report is the last one the Fed sees before it votes.

Oil's wild week: peace hopes, but tankers still under attack Oil fell about 13-15% this week to near $98 a barrel on hopes of a U.S.-Iran deal, the biggest weekly drop since 2022. But attacks on tankers in the Strait of Hormuz continue, and no deal has been signed, so if talks fail, oil could spike again and push inflation back up.

A bruising week, not a broken market The S&P 500 is still down 2.7% for the week and the Nasdaq sits in correction territory, 13% below its October record. About Mint. Mint looks for American stocks and ETFs that have already proven themselves — names that beat their benchmark over the past year. For stocks that benchmark is VGT, a technology index; for ETFs it's VOO, which tracks the S&P 500. Then Mint asks the harder question: which are most likely to keep winning without stumbling right after you buy?

The philosophy is simple: avoid losses first. We look past recent gains to the business behind each name, the risks that could derail it, and whether its success looks durable or depends on hype, headlines, or a one-time event. Strong recent performance gets a name noticed. Strong evidence earns it a spot. If nothing clears the bar, we leave the slot empty — every pick earns its place.

How it works, in three stages:

Find strength. Every stock and ETF is screened for beating its benchmark over the past year, then scored on the quality of the business, the timing of entry, and the steadiness of the ride. Hard risk gates throw out anything too volatile or too deeply scarred.

Question the story. Survivors get researched — we read the news behind each name, not just the numbers. How much does the story depend on things outside the company's control: wars, hype, one-time windfalls? Too much, and it's out on the spot. For the rest: how likely is the strength to continue, and how much do we trust our own read?

Weigh the odds. Will the strength continue, or reverse? Those judgments become one number — expected value: the likely gains if it holds, minus the likely pain if it turns, adjusted for how much we trust our own read.

Mint is built around a simple idea: find strength, question the story, weigh the odds, and only make room for what earns it.

Not financial advice. Past performance doesn't predict future returns. Also: there are gremlins that have control over the markets — they hate you personally, and they do the exact opposite of your buys and sells purely to spite you. Invest accordingly.
