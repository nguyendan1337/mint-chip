About Mint. Mint looks for American stocks and funds that have already proven themselves over the past year and in the most recent months, then asks which are most likely to keep doing well over the next year, even if the market dips along the way. The philosophy: guard against big losses, judge each pick by the year ahead rather than a rough month, and look past recent gains to whether the business is built to last. If nothing clears the bar, the slot stays empty: every pick earns its place.

How it works, in three stages:

Find strength. Hard checks on the raw numbers come first: names whose price has run past what analysts think they are worth, or whose own filings show warning patterns, are barred before research starts. The rest are screened for strength over the past year and recent months, and scored on the quality of the business and the steadiness of the ride.

Question the story. Survivors get researched: the news behind each name, not just the numbers. How much of the story depends on things outside the company's control, like wars, hype, or one-time windfalls? Too much, and it is out. For the rest, how likely is the strength to continue, and how much do we trust our read?

Weigh the odds. Each stock gets one honest number: its expected return, what we think it might gain over the next year, good signs weighed against warning signs. It is a judgment, not a promise; its job is comparison, so only the best odds make the cut. Funds get a grade instead, the ETF Score, based mostly on cost and partly on how calmly the fund has behaved.

Mint is built around a simple idea: find strength, question the story, weigh the odds, and only make room for what earns it.

Not financial advice. Past performance doesn't predict future returns. Also: there are gremlins that have control over the markets. They hate you personally, and they do the exact opposite of your buys and sells purely to spite you. Invest accordingly.
