Market today

Market's down. The broad market has been falling for five straight sessions and the S&P 500 sits at its lowest since July, mainly because interest rates are rising again and safe bonds now pay over 5%.

Rates are doing the damage The 10-year Treasury yield is back above 5% and traders see a roughly 95% chance the Fed raises rates again on December 9, with one Fed governor arguing for a bigger half-point move. When a safe government bond pays over 5%, it competes directly with stocks for money, so prices get marked down even on good news.

The Gulf war keeps oil expensive Tanker attacks in the Strait of Hormuz this week, plus strikes between the US and Iran, hold Brent crude near $93 a barrel. Expensive oil feeds inflation, and inflation is exactly what is pushing the Fed toward more rate hikes, so the war reaches stock prices twice.

The AI engine is still running Nvidia reported revenue up about 103% from a year ago and guided to about $130 billion next quarter, a sign that spending on AI computing is not slowing. Its stock faded during the day as rate worries took over, but the underlying demand behind the chip trade looks intact.

Shoppers are still spending Target posted its best sales growth in five years and TJX beat forecasts as holiday shopping starts strong, with sales this season projected to grow about 4 to 5%. A consumer still willing to spend is the strongest argument that this selloff is about rates, not about the economy cracking.

The bottom line This is a rate selloff, not a business selloff: earnings are coming in strong, but with the December Fed meeting ahead and PCE inflation data on November 25, caution rules until rates settle. Today's picks lean into that, favoring steady earners, health care, and cheaper large companies over anything that needs cheap money to work.

Mint looks for American stocks and funds that have already proven themselves, names beating their benchmark across a mix of recent windows: the past year, the past six months, and the past three months. For stocks that benchmark is VGT, a technology index; for funds it's VOO, which tracks the S&P 500. Then Mint asks the harder question: which are most likely to keep doing well over the next year, even if the market dips along the way?

The philosophy is simple: guard against big losses, but judge a pick by the year ahead, not by a rough month. We look past recent gains to the business behind each name, the risks that could derail it, and whether its success looks durable or depends on hype, headlines, or a one-time event. Strong recent performance gets a name noticed. Strong evidence earns it a spot. If nothing clears the bar, we leave the slot empty: every pick earns its place.

How it works, in three stages:

Find strength. Every stock and fund is screened for strength against its benchmark across those windows, then scored on the quality of the business, the timing of entry, and the steadiness of the ride. Hard risk gates bar names that have run too far too fast, fallen too hard, or swing too wildly.

Question the story. Survivors get researched. We read the news behind each name, not just the numbers. How much does the story depend on things outside the company's control: wars, hype, one-time windfalls? Too much, and it's out on the spot. For the rest: how likely is the strength to continue, and how much do we trust our own read?

Weigh the odds. For a stock, those judgments become one number, the expected return. It starts from a typical year's return, adds credit for things the company has actually done, like raising its dividend, buying back its own shares, or insiders buying with their own money, and subtracts for warning signs like a big acquisition or a buyback that only covered new shares handed out. The result is scaled by how much we trust our own read and how steady the business looks. A fund's figure is different: the ETF Score, a grade from 0 to 100 against fixed standards, based on what the fund costs, how big it is, and how calm its price has been lately, meaning how little it has spiked. It is not ranked against the other funds on the page, and it is not a forecast for that one fund. A fund needs at least 50 to be picked. Its bar is drawn on a scale of 200, so even the highest grade fills only half the bar.

Mint is built around a simple idea: find strength, question the story, weigh the odds, and only make room for what earns it.
